Alliance Selection

Draft your
alliance.

After the last qualification match, the top seeds take turns picking playoff partners. A few minutes, one list, and the whole playoff run gets decided. Today you are seed 2, and the scouting data is on the table.

The rankings just froze.

Qualification matches are over, and the scoreboard has done its sorting. Now comes alliance selection: the top-seeded teams become captains, and one by one they invite other teams to join them for the playoffs. It is like picking sides for a lunchtime game, except everyone saw every match, and the smart captains did their homework the night before.

To keep it fair, the picking order snakes. With four captains it runs 1-2-3-4, then turns around and runs 4-3-2-1. The captain who waited through the whole first round gets two picks back to back, the way you might let a friend take two pieces of candy in a row because you got to open the bag. Real FRC events run eight alliances instead of four, but the snake works exactly the same, and in this lesson we will play the four-alliance version so you can feel the whole thing in one sitting.

Captain

A top-seeded team that picks partners. You earn the chair by ranking well in quals.

Snake order

Round one runs down the seeds, round two runs back up. Waiting longest pays you back with back-to-back picks.

Pick list

A scouting-built ranking of every team, finished before selection starts. The list decides, not the moment.

The homework

Bring a list, not a feeling

Selection moves fast. A team gets called, the crowd reacts, the board changes, and suddenly it is your turn. If you try to think it through live, you will grab whatever robot looked flashy in the last match you happened to watch. Good teams walk in with a pick list: every team ranked, argued over the night before with the scouts, so that on stage the only job is to read the next name that is still available.

What goes on the list? Numbers, mostly. In ORBIT the scout table tracks each team's average Cells per match, which ring they usually feed, and how often they Balance in the endgame. From the first two you can compute a points-per-match estimate, the same idea as the stat called OPR that real scouting systems use. And the ring matters as much as the count: a mid-ring Cell is worth points in teleop and a high-ring Cell , so a team feeding 9 mid-ring Cells a match ( points) quietly outscores one making 5 high-ring Cells ( points), even though the high shots look cooler.

The hidden column

What a balancer is really worth

Here is the part most captains under-read. In the last seconds, a robot that Balances on the platform earns points. A robot that only parks earns . And when two or more alliance robots balance together, each of them adds the point Harmony bonus on top. So a team that balances 95 times out of 100 is quietly worth about endgame points every single match, before the bonus, while a 30 percent balancer averages closer to .

One more thing changes the moment quals end: ranking points stop existing. In the playoffs there is no Harmony RP to chase, no Energized RP, nothing but the match score. But do not toss the platform out with the RPs. Balance and the Harmony bonus are plain match points, and a near-certain pointer at the buzzer is worth more than a couple of flashy Cells that may or may not happen. Keep that thought. You are about to use it.

What the board missed

Value depends on who is asking

If you cleared the missions, you found it: the Lanterns are not a good team or a bad team, they are a good team for you. On Alliance 1, whose captain rarely balances, a lonely balancer collects their points and that is that. On your alliance, next to a captain who balances 85 percent of the time, the same robot keeps triggering the Harmony bonus, and the two of you together are worth more than you are apart. Scouts call a pick like that a sleeper: a team the headline stat undersells, waiting for the one captain who reads the whole table.

That is the deepest idea in alliance selection. You are not collecting the three biggest numbers, you are building one machine out of three robots. A second high scorer sounds great until both robots are jostling at the same loading bay. The draft rewards the team that asks what does my alliance need next, not who is best.

At a real event

Real selection has a few extra wrinkles: eight alliances instead of four, a captain may invite another captain to join them, and a team that declines an invitation cannot be picked by anyone else afterward, though it can still captain its own alliance. None of that changes the skill. The snake, the pick list, and reading value the other captains missed carry over exactly.

Going further · optional

Draft strategy has a second layer: knowing what will still be there later. If a team you like is ranked low on everyone else's board, picking them in round one wastes value, because they would have survived to your round-two pick anyway. Real scouts mark each target with "will they last until pick 7?" and reach early only when the answer is no. Here is one to chew on: Alliance 1's captain is the best scorer at this event. Why might their smartest first pick still be a balancer instead of the next-best scorer?

Show the thinking

Two scorers overlap: there is one Reactor and one loading bay per alliance, so a second scorer spends part of every match waiting behind the first, and its paper points shrink on the field. A balancer overlaps with nobody. It also fixes Alliance 1's real weakness, a captain who rarely balances, raising the alliance's floor with near-certain endgame points instead of stacking more of what they already have. The general rule: draft the robot that raises your worst number, not the one that pads your best number. And notice the draft-position layer too, since the second-best scorer may still be on the board at their last pick while the good balancers usually are not.

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
Four captains run a snake draft and you are seed 2. Round one goes 1-2-3-4. Which picks are yours?
The snake runs 1-2-3-4 then 4-3-2-1, so your picks are the 2nd and the 7th, and seed 4 gets picks 4 and 5 back to back as payment for waiting. Real events run the same snake with eight alliances.
Question 2
Why do teams build a pick list the night before selection instead of deciding on stage?
On stage there is noise, a ticking clock, and a board that changes with every name called. The pick list moves the hard thinking to the night before, when the scouts can argue it out with data. During selection the only job left is reading the highest name still available.
Question 3
From the sim: drafting the 8241 Lanterns, the lowest Reactor score on the board, projected better than drafting the 1773 Badgers, who out-score them. What made the difference?
A 95 percent balancer averages about endgame points before bonuses, while a 30 percent balancer averages closer to . Add the Harmony bonus next to your high-balance captain and the Lanterns swing more points at the buzzer than the Badgers add at the Reactor. The scout table's quietest column decided the match.
Question 4
Your team chased the Harmony ranking point all through quals. Ranking points vanish in the playoffs, so why does balancing still matter?
RPs only ever decided seeding, and seeding is settled. What survives into the playoffs is the scoring table itself: Balance still pays its points and Harmony still pays its bonus, as match points. That is why endgame reliability shows up on every good pick list.
