Scout Trainer
Companion · scouting

Be the team's eyes.

A robot wins or loses on the field, but the plan is built in the stands. Scouting is the job of watching every match, writing down what really happened, and turning a pile of numbers into one good decision. Three drills here train the whole loop: watch and tally a live match, read the sheet of gathered data, and build a pick list from a strategy brief. It all runs on ORBIT, so the skill sticks even when next January's game is nothing like this one.

Drill 1 / 3 Watch and tally

Count one robot

The first scouting job is the humblest: watch a match and write down what one robot did. Not the whole alliance, just your robot. You will be handed a single robot to follow. Every time it scores, tap the ring it hit. Every time it drives up and comes away empty, tap Missed. Keep your eyes on the glowing rose dot and let the other five blur into traffic.

Predict first
Before you watch: with one robot to follow and five others moving, what actually trips up a new scout the most?
Following the dot is the easy part. The hard part is the split second the Cell arrives: did it land on a ring or bounce off, and was it the inner, middle, or outer ring? At speed those calls blur together, and a miss counted as a make quietly wrecks the sheet. Run it and see how tight your count comes out.

Drill 2 / 3 Read the data

Read the sheet

By the last day of qualifications the scouts have a full sheet: every team's average Cells per match, which ring they feed, how often they Balance, and their best and worst match so you can see how steady they are. The skill now is reading it right. The biggest number in a column is not always the answer, and the quietest column often decides a match. Answer each question by clicking the team's row.

Going further · optional

Real scouting has a step this trainer hides from you: trust. One scout watching one robot will slip - a miscounted miss, a match they zoned out for. Good teams fix that by having two or three people scout the same robot and then comparing sheets, throwing out the numbers that do not agree. They also track things a single number cannot hold: did the robot break down, did it play defense well, was it fast at the loading bay. Here is a question worth chewing on before you peek.

Two teams both average 8 Cells a match. One ranges from 7 to 9, the other from 1 to 15. Their averages are identical. Which one do you want on your alliance for a playoff match, and why might the answer flip if you were the underdog with nothing to lose?

Show the thinking

For a normal playoff match you almost always want the steady 7-to-9 team. Playoffs are best-of-three against a known opponent, so you are planning a score you can count on, and a robot that might give you 1 is a robot that loses you a match you had planned to win. The wild 1-to-15 team adds risk you cannot control. But flip the seats: if you are the low seed facing an alliance that will beat you on any average day, a steady team just loses steadily. Now you want variance - you need the 15-Cell match to show up and the favorite to have an off day at the same time. Underdogs buy lottery tickets on purpose. The same number, consistency, is an asset or a liability depending entirely on whether you are ahead or behind. That is the deepest idea in scouting: a stat only means something once you know what job you are asking it to do.

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
Why does a scout tally one assigned robot instead of trying to watch the whole alliance at once?
Accuracy comes from focus. A scout locked onto one robot catches every score and every miss; a scout sweeping all six misses half of each. The team covers all robots by handing each scout a different one, so every robot gets watched closely by somebody.
Question 2
One team on your sheet logs the most Cells per match, but you rank a different team higher for scoring. What are you seeing that the Cell count hides?
Reactor points are Cells times ring value, not raw Cells. A high-ring Cell pays points and a low-ring Cell pays , so a team firing 7 high-ring Cells out-scores one dumping 13 low-ring ones. The count column is a decoy; the points column is the truth.
Question 3
Your captain is a great high-ring scorer who almost never balances. Two teams are on the board: a huge high-ring scorer, and a steady mid-ring team that balances 90 percent of the time. Why might the smaller scorer be the better pick?
Value depends on who is asking. Next to a captain who already owns the high ring, a second high scorer overlaps - they crowd the same ring and the same loading bay, so the extra Cells do not all land. The balancer overlaps nobody and patches the captain's one weakness, adding a near-certain -point climb every match. Pick the robot that raises your worst number, not the one that pads your best.
