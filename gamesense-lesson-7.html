The Strategy Huddle

Three robots,
one plan.

The draft gave you two partners. Now the three of you get about two minutes in the queue line to agree on what each robot does with its seconds. This is the meeting where matches get won early.

The bracket is out.

Say your draft went the way the gold mission did last lesson: you are the 6072 Fireflies, and sitting beside you are the 7688 Orioles and the 8241 Lanterns. Somewhere ahead in the bracket waits Alliance 1 - the Thunderheads, the Comets they grabbed with the first pick, and the Badgers they scooped up at the end. Your scouts have already written down exactly how Alliance 1 plays. What nobody has written down yet is how you will.

That is what the strategy huddle is for. Before every playoff match, the three drive teams find each other near the queue and talk. A good huddle is not a pep talk, and it is not three teams each promising to do their best. It ends with a plan: one job per robot, plus a clock that says when each job changes.

Think of a long group ride. Three riders who all sprint at once burn out and finish slow. A group that works takes turns: one rider pulls at the front and breaks the wind, the others tuck in behind and save their legs, and everyone knows when the switch happens. Nobody is being generous. The group is simply faster when the jobs are split, and the riders agreed on the split before the road got hard. Your alliance is that group now.

The jobs

A plan is three choices

In the planner below, each of your robots takes exactly one job: cycle the high, mid, or low ring, feed, defend, or dock early. Three of those jobs score Cells. Three of them score nothing at all, and this lesson exists because the nothing-jobs keep winning matches.

First, a warning about the scoring jobs. Your alliance shares one loading bay and one face of each Reactor ring. Put two robots on the same ring and they queue behind each other at both ends - in the planner, every lap on a shared ring stretches by seconds per robot. Remember the draft lesson's warning about two scorers jostling at one loading bay? Today you get to watch it cost you points.

Feed

Hoover Cells and hand them to your partners. Scores zero itself, but every scoring teammate's lap gets seconds shorter. ORBIT's scouting notes call this the Feeder archetype.

Defend

Shadow their most dangerous scorer. Their lap stretches by seconds, but expect to concede about one foul ( points) - pinning is rule .

Dock early

Head home with seconds still on the clock. The Balance is locked in, and a settled platform nudges each teammate's balance odds up about points in a hundred.

The clock

The endgame starts in the middle

You know the shape of a match from Part 1: seconds of auto, of teleop, and the final seconds are the endgame window. A robot on the standard plan cycles until that window opens, then rides home to the platform - which means its real scoring day is only seconds long. Every projection in the planner is built on that window.

Docking early moves one robot's departure up to seconds remaining. That costs about a lap of scoring, and here is what it buys. A Balance pays points against for a park, so an endgame that goes wrong is expensive - and rule says that once the last seconds begin, opponents may not touch a robot in contact with its own platform. Arrive early and you are already untouchable when it matters, with the platform settled for the teammates arriving behind you. When two or more of you balance together, each adds the point Harmony bonus on top.

One more idea before you take the whiteboard, and it is the biggest one in this lesson. The scoreboard only ever shows a difference. A point you delete from their column is worth exactly as much as a point you add to yours. Keep that in your pocket for the moment a zero-scoring job starts to look useless.

Debrief

What the big wins share

If you cleared the missions, you noticed something strange. Out of every possible all-scoring plan, exactly one of them wins, and it squeaks by with less than a point to spare. Every comfortable win on that board benches a scorer. The best plan of all puts the Lanterns on feed: they hand over their own points of low-ring scoring, and their shuttle service gives the Fireflies and Orioles about points of extra Cells between them. The alliance got stronger because one robot agreed to disappear from the scoring column.

Defense tells the same story from the other side. The Lanterns shadowing the Thunderheads erase points of high-ring scoring at the cost of one conceded foul - and since the scoreboard only shows a difference, deleting their points counts exactly like scoring your own. Feed grows your column, defense shrinks theirs, and dock early trades a lap for certainty. Three different jobs, one shared idea: a robot's value is what happens to the margin, not what appears next to its own name.

At a real event

The real huddle happens in the queue line with the next match's teams, and it is short. Good ones sound like this: one voice per team, numbers instead of adjectives ("we average nine mid Cells, you take high"), and a plan B agreed out loud before anyone needs it - "if their defender latches onto our high robot, we swap rings and keep rolling." Deciding the swap now costs nothing. Deciding it mid-match, over a crackling headset, costs a lap or two every time.

Going further · optional

The planner trusts the scout report exactly, but real robots have good days. Suppose the Thunderheads run hot and lap in seconds instead of - that is extra points for Alliance 1. Which of your winning plans survives that surprise: the all-scoring squeaker, or the big-margin feed plan? Work out both new margins before you peek. This is why drive coaches chase margin instead of stopping at "projected to win."

Margin is not showing off - it is armor against the day the scout report runs hot. Statisticians would say the projection is an expected value and real matches have variance around it; a plan that only wins when every number lands exactly is not really a winning plan. The wider your margin, the more surprise the plan can absorb and still come home ahead.
