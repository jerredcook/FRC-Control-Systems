Beyond the single CAD lesson

Make the sketch stop wiggling

The CAD lesson showed you how to sketch a shape and pull it into a solid. This companion goes one layer deeper into the thing that trips up every new CAD designer: a sketch that floppes around because it is not pinned down. You will drive a real sketch from loose to locked by adding constraints, watch its degrees of freedom fall to zero, then learn how the same idea joins parts together in an assembly. Onshape is the free, browser-based CAD our Fabricate course already teaches.

By the end you can
  • Say what "degrees of freedom" means and read the DOF count as you constrain a sketch
  • Drive a loose sketch to fully defined, and spot when you are over-constraining it
  • Name the common assembly mates from the motion they allow
The idea

A sketch has freedom until you take it away

Draw a rectangle roughly and its corners can still slide anywhere.

Every point you draw starts free: it can move in x and in y. Two numbers of freedom per point. A four-corner plate has eight numbers of freedom before you pin anything down. Those eight are its degrees of freedom, or DOF. Grab a corner and it floppes, because nothing is holding it.

A constraint removes freedom. You add them one at a time until there is nothing left to wiggle:

📍
CoincidentPin one point onto another point, or onto the origin. That locks both its x and its y at once, so it removes 2 DOF.
📐
Horizontal / VerticalForce an edge flat or upright. It removes 1 DOF - the edge can no longer tilt.
📏
DimensionType a real number, like 120 mm, onto a length or a gap. It removes 1 DOF - that size is now fixed.
✅
Fully definedDOF hits 0. Nothing can move. Onshape turns the sketch a solved color, and it will never drift on you again.

The goal is simple: get the DOF to zero. Not more, not less. Below zero is a real problem too, and you will meet it in a second.

Each constraint takes away a way the plate could move. Pinning A to the origin removes two at once; each edge or dimension removes one. When the last one lands, DOF is 0 and the wiggle stops for good.

🎯 Missions your goals for this sim - take them at your own pace
○Add your first constraint and watch the DOF drop below 8. That is one way the plate can no longer move.
○Keep going until DOF hits 0. The plate turns green and stops flopping - that is a fully defined sketch, the state every real part wants to be in.
○Now poke the danger: click a constraint that is already implied (try Pin A to origin twice, or a dimension you already set). Read the over-constrained warning - real Onshape refuses it the same way.
★Gold: reach DOF 0 in the minimum 7 constraints. The trick: pin A to the origin (that single coincident kills 2 DOF at once) instead of dimensioning A.x and A.y separately, which costs you a wasted move.
Under-defined, defined, over-defined

Three states, and only one you want. Under-defined (DOF above 0) means the sketch can still drift, and it will move on you when you edit something nearby - the classic source of "why did my whole part just warp?" Fully defined (DOF exactly 0) is the goal: solid, predictable, safe to build features on. Over-defined (you tried to remove freedom that was already gone) means two constraints are fighting over the same thing, and Onshape flags it in red so you can delete the extra one. Aim for zero, land on zero.

One level up

From one part to a whole robot

A single fully-defined part is pinned inside its own sketch. But a robot is many parts, and now each part has DOF relative to the parts around it. A loose part in an assembly floats in space with 6 degrees of freedom: it can slide along 3 directions and spin about 3 axes.

In an assembly you remove that freedom with mates. A mate is a constraint between two parts, and each kind leaves behind a different, deliberate amount of motion. That leftover motion is the whole point - it is how your mechanism moves:

🔩
Fastened0 DOF left. Bolted solid, no motion. A gearbox screwed to a side plate.
🔄
Revolute1 rotation. Spins about one axis only. An arm on a pivot, a wheel on a shaft.
↕️
Slider1 translation. Slides along one straight line. An elevator carriage on its rails.
🌀
Cylindrical1 rotation + 1 slide, same axis. A telescoping tube that spins and extends.
🔮
Ball3 rotations, no slide. Swivels every direction about one point, like a joystick.
🛝
Planar2 slides + 1 spin on a surface. A part resting flat on a plate.

Reading a mate is a skill you can train: look at the joint, ask "what motion does it still allow?", and the answer names the mate. Let us drill it.

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
In a 2D sketch, what does the degrees-of-freedom number count?
DOF is the count of independent motions still left in the sketch. Each free point starts with 2 (x and y), and every constraint you add subtracts from that total until it reaches 0.
Question 2
You pin one corner of a plate onto the origin with a single coincident constraint. How much freedom does that one move remove?
A point has 2 DOF, x and y. Landing it exactly on the origin fixes both at once, so one coincident removes 2. That is why pinning to the origin is the cheapest way to anchor a sketch, and why the gold mission wanted it instead of two separate dimensions.
Question 3
Onshape flashes an "over-constrained" warning at you. What did you most likely do?
Over-constrained means you tried to remove freedom that was already gone - two constraints now describe the same thing, so they either duplicate or conflict. The fix is to delete the extra one and get back to exactly 0 DOF.
Question 4
An elevator carriage rides two rails: it slides straight up and down and does nothing else. Which assembly mate is that?
A slider leaves exactly 1 translation - motion along one straight line, and nothing else. That is precisely a carriage on rails. Revolute would leave a rotation, and cylindrical would leave a rotation plus the slide.
