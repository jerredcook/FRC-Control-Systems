Electrical triage

The eight-minute pit

A robot rolls back from the field with something dead. You have one battery, a driver's account of what happened, and a countdown before your alliance is called again. Triage is looking before you leap: read the panel, form a hunch, and spend your seconds only where they buy an answer. This clinic drops a real electrical fault into a robot and lets you practice that read, safely, as many times as you want.

The read

What the panel is telling you

Almost every "it just died" in the pit is one of a handful of electrical faults, and each leaves a signature you can learn to spot at a glance.

The Driver Station lights are your first look. Comms is the radio link between the laptop and the robot. Code is the robot program actually running. Joysticks is your controllers being seen. Next to them sits the battery voltmeter, and inside the robot the main breaker (the big 120 A switch) and a bank of branch breakers, one small Snap-Action per motor controller.

Tripped breaker

A branch Snap-Action popped mid-match and killed power to one controller. Everything else runs. The breaker is warm and its button is out.

Dead PDH channel

Looks just like a tripped breaker - one subsystem dark - but the breaker is fine. The channel itself stopped passing power. Move the device to a spare channel and it wakes up.

Loose CAN connection

The controller still has power and its LED is lit, but it fell off the CAN bus. It blinks a fault pattern and vanishes from the device scan. Wiggle the connector and it flickers back.

Sagged battery

Not one subsystem - the whole robot browns out and reboots under load. The pack can read okay at rest and still collapse the instant you draw current.

The method

Spend seconds to buy answers

You cannot pull every wire in eight minutes, and yanking connectors at random usually makes things worse. Triage is a loop:

  1. Read the panel and the driver's report. Lights, voltmeter, and what the driver felt tell you a lot before you touch anything.
  2. Form a hunch. A dead single subsystem points at a power path. A whole-robot brownout points at the battery. A device that shows up in code as disconnected points at CAN.
  3. Probe to confirm or rule out. Each probe below costs pit-time. Pick the one that separates your top two suspects, not the one that just repeats what the panel already shows.
  4. Commit the call before the clock runs out. A confident, mostly-right read beats a perfect read that arrives after the buzzer.

How the pit clock works

You start each case with a fictional 8:00 pit window. It ticks down in real time, and every probe you run spends a chunk of it - a quick Driver Station read is cheap, hauling a device onto a spare channel is not. That is the real trade in a pit: information costs time, so you buy only what moves your decision. A tripped breaker and a dead channel look identical on the panel, so those cases are the ones that reward a smart probe. Nothing here is graded harshly - a wrong call just shows you the tell and lets you reset. Your progress saves in this browser.

Safety - this is real current

On an actual robot, the battery and the Power Distribution Hub carry enough current to burn skin, melt wire, and start a fire. Always work on the electrical system with your mentor, disconnect the battery before touching wiring, and never reset a breaker that keeps popping without finding out what is stalling behind it. This clinic is for the read - the hands-on fixes belong on a powered-down robot.

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
The robot drives fine, comms and code are both green, and the battery load-tests healthy - but the intake is dead. What is the first thing to check?
With comms, code, and battery all good but one mechanism dead, the power path to that single controller is the prime suspect - and a tripped Snap-Action breaker is the most common cause. Check the breaker first.
Question 2
A tripped breaker and a dead PDH channel look identical on the panel - one subsystem dead, everything else green. How do you tell them apart?
A tripped Snap-Action is popped and warm; a dead channel's breaker is seated and cool. The clincher is moving the device to a spare channel - if it wakes up with a good breaker still on the original, the channel itself was dead.
Question 3
A motor controller's LED is lit but blinking a fault pattern, and the device is missing from the CAN scan. What is going on?
Power reaching the controller (LED lit) plus a CAN-fault blink and a missing device on the scan is the signature of a loose CAN wire. Wiggle the connector and it usually flickers back onto the bus.
Question 4
The battery reads about 11.8 V at rest, but the robot browns out and reboots whenever the driver accelerates hard. Best move?
A resting voltage can look fine while a worn battery collapses the moment you draw current, causing brownouts and reboots. The fix is a fresh, fully charged pack - and set the tired one aside with your mentor.
