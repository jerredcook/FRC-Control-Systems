Scoring Math

Count laps,
not vibes.

One stopwatch number tells you what a robot is really worth. Turn cycle time into points, and points into a plan your whole alliance can trust.

Strategy talk gets loud. Math settles it.

Watch any ORBIT match and one robot "looks fast" while another "looks clutch". Feelings are fun, but the scoreboard does not run on feelings. It runs on one repeating loop: a robot grabs a Cell, drives to the Reactor, scores it, and heads back for the next one. That loop is a cycle, and the seconds one loop takes is the robot's cycle time.

Think of a paper route on a bike. You do not guess how long the route takes. You time one lap of the block and multiply. Robots are the same. Teleop gives every robot the same 135 seconds, so divide by the cycle time and you know exactly how many trips fit.

cycles = teleop seconds ÷ cycle time always round down. a lap that ends at the buzzer with the Cell still on the robot scores nothing

So a robot with a quick 15 second lap finishes 9 full cycles, while a robot that dawdles for 27 seconds a lap manages only 5. Nobody argued. Nobody guessed. The stopwatch did all the work.

The currency

A cycle is worth its ring

Not every trip pays the same. In ORBIT the Reactor has three rings, and each one has its own price tag. Here they are, straight from the manual:

Low ring

2 points per Cell in teleop, 4 in auto. Easy to reach, quick to feed.

Mid ring

5 points per Cell in teleop, 10 in auto. The steady middle path.

High ring

8 points per Cell in teleop, 16 in auto. Big pay, hard shot.

Notice auto pays double for the same ring, which is why teams fight so hard for those opening seconds. But here is the trap: "the high ring pays the most, so the high ring is best, right?" Not so fast. A ring is only as good as how quickly you can feed it. The number that actually settles arguments is the rate:

points per second = ring points ÷ cycle time the one number that lets you compare any two robots, any two rings

Try it on two ORBIT regulars. A low-ring feeder scoring 2 points every 10 seconds earns about 0.20 points a second. A mid-ring all-arounder scoring 5 every 18 earns about 0.28. The feeder looks busier, buzzing back and forth all match, but the slower robot is quietly out-earning it. Your eyes vote for hustle. The math votes for rate.

The clock

Spend the last seconds on purpose

One more wrinkle before you get the calculator. The final 30 seconds of teleop are the endgame, and the platform in your corner pays for how well you finish: 5 for a park, 10 for a dock, 20 for a full balance. Those seconds get spent either way, so spend them on purpose. A high-ring robot can squeeze about 2 more cycles into that window, worth 16 points. Or it can ride home and balance for 20. Scoring to the buzzer feels right and pays worse.

And your robot's math never stands alone. An alliance is three robots, and their totals add. ORBIT even hands out a bonus ranking point called Energized when an alliance scores at least 40 Reactor points, meaning points from the rings themselves. Three ordinary robots with a plan can bank that RP every single match. Three great robots without a plan can miss it. That is why this lesson exists.

Why it matters

The stopwatch beats the highlight reel

Everything you just did with sliders works at a real event with a phone. Time three of a robot's cycles from the stands, average them, and you know its rate. Do that for six robots and you can sketch both alliances' totals before the match even starts. When your drive coach says "we need their mid robot to go two seconds faster or we balance early," that is not a hunch. That is this lesson.

Talk in numbers, land the plan

"You guys looked fast" starts a friendly chat. "You average a 14 second high cycle, so together we clear Energized with time to balance" starts a partnership. In the strategy huddle later in this course, the team that shows up with cycle math usually writes the plan everyone else follows.

Going further · optional

The calculator quietly assumes every shot lands. Real robots miss. Suppose your high-ring robot (14 s cycles) makes 4 of every 5 shots, while a 10 s mid-ring robot never misses. Who really wins teleop now? Work it out with expected points: cycles × ring points × accuracy. Then peek.

Show the thinking

The high robot still runs its 9 cycles, but only 4 in 5 pay out, so its expected total drops to about 57.6. The mid robot keeps its full 65 and wins. The "weaker" robot on paper is the stronger robot in practice, because accuracy multiplies every single cycle. This is exactly why good scouts track makes and misses, not just speed, and it is the first taste of a stats idea called expected value that you will meet again when we talk about pick lists.

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
Teleop lasts 135 seconds and your robot's cycle takes 20 seconds. How many scoring trips does it finish?
Cycles always round down. 135 over 20 is 6.75, and the leftover fraction of a lap ends at the buzzer with the Cell still aboard, worth zero. Planning around trips that never finish is how alliances come up short by "just a few points".
Question 2 · From the sim
In the calculator, Robot A (high ring, 14 s cycles) beat Robot B (mid ring, 10 s cycles), 72 to 65. What single number explains the win?
Rate is the referee. A earns about 0.57 points a second and B about 0.50, so A wins even while making fewer trips. Whenever two strategies argue, divide each one's points by its seconds and the argument ends.
Question 3
There are 30 seconds left and your high-ring robot (14 s cycles) can keep scoring or head home to balance. Which plan pays more?
Run the numbers, not the adrenaline: 2 late cycles pay 16 while a Balance pays 20, so heading home early wins by a few points. You watched this exact flip in the calculator, and a few points is a whole match more often than you would believe.
Question 4
Which of these earns the Energized ranking point in ORBIT?
Energized takes at least 40 Reactor points, points scored on the rings, from all three robots combined. That is alliance math, not solo math, which is why the planner adds three robots together. Even a plan with a full-time defender can clear it, as the gold mission proves.
