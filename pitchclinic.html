Pitch clinic

A robot wins matches. A team wins awards, sponsors, and the room. This is the half of FRC that happens off the field: talking to judges, asking a business for help, and telling your story. Good news for the quiet folks - this is exactly where you find out that you contribute too, and that your steady behind-the-scenes job is worth just as much as the loudest voice.

Before you practice

Three rooms, one skill

The business half of FRC lives in three rooms, and the same skill wins all three: say something specific and true, in your own voice, and stop.

Judges

At every event, judges walk the pits and ask questions. Awards like Impact and Engineering Inspiration are decided here, not on the field. They are listening for real people doing real things.

Sponsors

Robots cost money. Businesses help when the ask is clear: who you are, what the money does, and what they get back. It is a trade, not a favor.

Outreach

The thing you give back to your community - a club you run, kids you mentor, an event you host. It is the story judges and sponsors care about most.

Here is the whole trick, and it is smaller than it looks. Weak answers all fail the same three ways: they are vague ("we all worked hard"), they brag or blame (lifting yourself by pushing a teammate down), or they ramble until the point is buried. Strong answers are specific, honest, and short. That is a skill, and skills get better with reps. So let us do some reps.

If talking to strangers scares you

That is normal, and you are not alone. You do not need to become a loud person. You need one true sentence about the real thing you do - the wiring map you keep, the build log nobody else updates, the pit you organized. Naming your own work plainly is not bragging. It is the honest answer to an honest question, and by the end of this page you will have that sentence ready. Practice it out loud with a mentor before your first event.

Drill 2

Why the "bad" ones sink you

A full technical spec answers a question the sponsor did not ask - they want to know what their money does, not every bracket's part number. A guilt line ("if you do not help, we cannot compete") pressures people and pushes them away; sponsors give to a clear, confident ask. And personal contact info like a student's home address should never go on a document you hand out - keep contact to a mentor or team email. Safety first, always with your mentor.

Drill 3

Your own words

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
A judge asks what you personally did this season. What makes an answer land?
Judges are listening for real people doing real things. One specific job, in your own voice, that only you can claim - that is the answer they remember. "We" answers and put-downs both fail; specific and honest wins.
Question 2
What belongs on a one-page sponsorship ask?
A sponsorship is a trade. The one-pager makes it easy to say yes: this is us, this is what your money does, this is what you get, here is how to reach us. Clear and confident beats long and technical every time.
Question 3
A shy teammate says they do not really contribute. What is the kindest, truest thing to help them see?
A team is made of many jobs, and the quiet ones hold it together. Helping a shy teammate name their real contribution is how they find out they belong here - and it is the honest truth, not a pep talk.
You did the reps

Awards and sponsors are not won by the flashiest robot. They are won by a team that can look someone in the eye and say one specific, honest, generous thing. You just practiced that. Read your three sentences out loud once, then go find a mentor and say them to a real person - that last rep is the one that sticks.
