The call is yours

Building a robot is a people problem wearing a metal costume. Here are five real build-season dilemmas - the kind that stall a whole team for a week. You pick what you would do, and the clinic tells you the likely outcome. There is rarely one perfect answer, but there are clearly better and worse calls, and this is a safe place to make the bad ones.

First, the shape of it

What a subteam lead actually does

A subteam - intake, drivetrain, electrical, programming - is a small group owning one job. Leading it is less about being the best builder and more about keeping people and parts unblocked.

Two ideas run under every scenario below. The first is people: the best idea has to be able to reach the room, no matter who has the quietest voice, and no one person should be a wall the whole team crashes into. The second is task dependencies: some work can only start once other work is done. Draw those arrows and the schedule almost writes itself. Miss them and you get two subteams politely waiting on each other while the clock runs out.

Protect a baseline

Always keep something that works. Improve in parallel against a deadline instead of betting the season on an untested redo.

Make work visible

Files, schedules, and decisions live where the whole team can see them. Invisible work turns one person into a bottleneck.

Sequence by dependency

Whoever's work others are waiting on goes first. A shared, posted plan beats a scramble every single time.

Run the room

Part of leading a meeting is traffic control - making sure the best idea gets out, not just the loudest voice.

Running a subteam · the through-line

Every scenario you just worked is the same two ideas in a different jersey. Keep these on a sticky note when it is your call:

  • Never ship without a fallback. A working robot that jams sometimes still beats a beautiful redo that is not done. Prototype the upgrade on the side, with a deadline and a "must clearly beat the old one" bar.
  • Make the work visible. Shared drive, posted schedule, decisions written down. The moment progress lives on one laptop or in one person's head, that person becomes a wall the whole team hits.
  • Draw the dependency arrows. Ask "what has to be done before this can start?" for every task. Whoever others wait on goes first, and the plan gets posted where everyone can see it.
  • Break circular waits at the boundary. When two subteams each wait on the other, they are missing a shared agreement, not motion. Get both leads to define and freeze the interface - the bolt pattern, the mount, the signal - then both build to it at once.
  • Protect the quiet voice. Hand the floor to the person getting talked over, by name. Do it a few times and the room starts doing it on its own. The goal is the best idea winning, not the loudest.

None of this needs you to be the best builder in the room. It needs you to keep people and parts unblocked, and to bring a mentor in early when a call is bigger than you.

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
Your intake jams about 1 in 5 tries and the lead wants a full rebuild with two weeks of build season left. What is the safest way to allow the improvement?
Protect a baseline, then improve against it. A working robot plus a side prototype with a deadline gets you the upgrade if it lands and a robot either way. That is the whole trick of changing things late.
Question 2
One designer keeps every part file on their own laptop, and the team stalls whenever they are out. The real fix is:
Single points of failure are a process problem, not a personality one. A shared drive with a WIP convention makes work visible and safe at the same time, so nobody can become a wall by going home.
Question 3
The arm subteam is waiting on the frame subteam for the side rail, and the frame subteam is waiting on the arm subteam for the exact bolt pattern. Nothing moves. What breaks the deadlock?
A circular wait is a missing agreement, not missing motion. Define the interface - the bolt pattern and where the rail meets the gearbox - freeze it so it cannot drift, and now neither side has to wait on the other.
Question 4
A quiet rookie keeps getting talked over in design meetings but seems to have spotted a real problem with the shooter. Best move:
A leader's job in a meeting is partly traffic control. Naming the person and handing them the floor gets the idea out and teaches the room a norm. Do it a few times and it starts self-correcting.
Question 5
It is integration week and electrical, programming, and mechanical all grab the robot at once, so nothing gets finished. What helps most?
A visible schedule with owners and a definition of done turns a scramble into a queue. People plan their work around their slot instead of hovering, and whoever others depend on goes first.
