A real robot has weight, so it does not stop the instant you do. Practice that feel here on the ORBIT field: three timed drills, best times that stick, and a robot that carries its momentum just like the real thing.

Keyboard or controller

The keyboard works fine - drive with WASD or the arrow keys. But if you plug in a game controller, the lab will find it and switch to the left stick, and a real controller feels a lot more like driving the real thing: push a little for a little speed, push all the way for full speed.

Driving is a skill you practice, not a thing you're born knowing.

The best drive teams log hours on the practice field before a competition, because the robot never does exactly what your thumbs expect. It has mass, so it keeps rolling after you let go, and it takes a moment to get up to speed. The only way to make that feel automatic is repetition - and repetition is a lot more fun with a clock running.

This lab drops you on a top-down ORBIT field. The Reactor tower sits at center, your loading bay is on the left wall, and your platform waits in the corner. Three drills train three different driving skills: weaving, cycling, and stopping on a dime. Beat your own times, then beat the tight ones.

Slalom

Weave through five markers in order. Trains smooth, committed steering - the thing defenders make hard.

Cycle Run

Loading bay to Reactor and back, three times. Trains the round trip that scores every point in a real match.

Precision Park

Stop fully inside the platform box, three times. Trains the endgame park, where hurrying costs you.

Predict first
You're driving at full speed straight toward the loading bay, then you let go of the stick completely. What does the robot do?
A robot has mass, and mass means momentum: when you stop asking for speed, the robot coasts to a halt instead of freezing in place. That glide is exactly what makes precise stopping hard, and it is why the Precision Park drill exists. Drive at full tilt, release, and watch how far it drifts before the speed readout reaches zero.

What you're training

Every drill is a real match habit

These aren't arcade games for their own sake. Each one drills a moment you'll actually live in a real match, and the ORBIT scoreboard rewards all three.

The Cycle Run is the whole sport in miniature. Points only land when the robot travels from where it picks up Cells to where it scores them and comes back for more. Shave a second off each round trip and, over a two-and-a-half minute match, you fit in an extra cycle or two - which can be the difference on the scoreboard. The flat stretches where you're just driving are pure cost, so the fastest, cleanest path wins.

Precision Park is the endgame. On ORBIT, settling your robot onto its platform is worth up to 20 points - as much as a whole handful of cycles - but only if you actually stop inside the box before the buzzer. Overcook it and slide off the edge and you get nothing. That is why stopping cleanly, against the robot's momentum, is a skill worth drilling until it's boring.

Read the glide

Watch the speed readout as you practice Precision Park. The trick is not to brake hard at the box - it is to ease off early and let the robot's own momentum carry it to a stop right on the mark. A good driver plans the stop a full robot-length before they get there, the same way you coast a bike up to a stop sign instead of slamming the brakes.

Going further · optional

Here is a puzzle to chew on before you peek. On the keyboard, a key is either fully pressed or fully released - there is no in-between. On a controller, the stick reads every position from a feather-touch to all the way over. Why does that analog range make precise driving, especially the Precision Park stop, noticeably easier? Think about what your options are for slowing down with each input.

Show the thinking

With the keyboard you only have two speeds: full throttle or coasting. To slow down, your only real move is to let go and drift, or tap the opposite key to lurch backward - both are blunt. The analog stick gives you the whole middle. You can hold the robot at one meter per second and creep the last few centimeters into the box, or feather the throttle down as you approach so the momentum bleeds off exactly at the line. More choices between "go" and "stop" means finer control, which is why almost every FRC drive team uses a gamepad or joysticks, not a keyboard, and why the gold times here are set with a controller in mind.

Checkpoint

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
In the lab, when you release the stick at full speed the robot keeps rolling for a moment before it stops. What is that modeling?
A real robot weighs tens of kilograms, and mass carries momentum. When you stop asking for speed, that momentum keeps it rolling until friction and braking bleed it off. The lab copies that on purpose, because learning to plan around the glide is most of what makes a driver precise.
Question 2 · From the drills
The Cycle Run has you drive the loading bay to the Reactor and back three times. In a real match, what does driving a shorter, cleaner path between those two spots buy your alliance?
A match is a fixed length, so every second you save on the round trip is a second you can spend on another cycle. Points only land when the robot completes that bay-to-Reactor-and-back loop, so a faster, cleaner path directly means more Cells scored before the buzzer. That is why drive teams obsess over their routes.
Question 3
The lab says a real controller feels closer to driving the actual robot than the keyboard does. Why?
A keyboard key has two states: pressed or not, full speed or coasting. A thumbstick reports its full range, so you can ask for a crawl, a cruise, or a sprint and anything between. That fine control is exactly what real robots give their drivers, and it is why easing the stick down for a clean Precision Park stop is so much easier than stabbing keys.
