Question 1
An alliance fields one Scorer and two Feeders. On the scoreboard the two Feeders barely register. What makes that lineup so dangerous?
Fed cycles multiply, they do not add. Two Feeders shrink the Scorer's lap until one robot delivers Cells like two, which is why the whole alliance total lives in a single robot - and why one Defender can unravel it. Review: Part 2.1 · Robot Roles
Question 2
You chased the Harmony ranking point all through qualifications. Ranking points stop existing in the playoffs, so why would you still spend a pick on a team that Balances almost every match?
Ranking points only ever decided seeding, and seeding is frozen. Balance () and the Harmony bonus ( each when partners climb together) are ordinary match points, so a near-certain climb often beats a couple of flashy Cells. That is why a reliable balancer with a low Reactor score - the sleeper everyone scrolls past - can be your best pick. Review: Part 2.2 · Alliance Selection
Question 3
Your two best robots both cycle the high ring fastest, so the huddle opens with both of them there. Sliding one down to the mid ring, which pays fewer points per Cell, actually raises your projected score. Why?
High pays the most per Cell ( against on mid), but two robots stacked on one ring jam the same lane and the same loading bay, so both laps grow. Split across two rings and the extra Cells delivered beat the higher per-Cell value - the same jam the draft lesson warned about when two scorers crowd one loading bay. Review: Part 2.3 · The Strategy Huddle
Question 4
You swap a scoring robot for a Defender that collects zero Cells. Your own total drops, and yet the projection flips from a loss to a win. What is the general lesson?
The scoreboard only ever shows a difference. A Defender scores nothing itself but can erase far more than the offense you gave up, and a careless one hands back only points per foul. Weigh a role by how it moves the margin, then run the numbers fresh every match. Review: Part 2.3 · The Strategy Huddle
