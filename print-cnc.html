Print and CNC Strategy
Manufacturing strategy

Print it, mill it,
or just buy it.

A 3D printer feels like magic until a part snaps in your hand at a competition. Printed plastic is strong one way and weak another, and the direction you print it in decides which. This page lets you rotate a part against a pull test and watch it go from rock-solid to falling apart, then trains the bigger call: print, machine, or buy.

By the end you can
  • Explain why a 3D printed part is weakest along its layer lines, and orient a part so the load runs along the layers instead of across them.
  • Read a pull-test verdict and say which orientation is strongest and which is weakest, without guessing.
  • Look at a part and pick the right process for it: 3D print, CNC machine, or buy off the shelf.

A 3D printer builds a part out of stacked layers.

A common shop printer (called FDM) squirts out a thin thread of hot plastic, draws one flat layer with it, lifts up a hair, and draws the next layer on top. Your part grows upward as a stack of these layers, a little like building something out of a stack of sticky notes, or the way tree rings stack up. Look closely at any printed part and you can see the layer lines running across it.

Here is the catch that surprises people: the plastic inside one layer is fully melted and strong, but the bond between two stacked layers is weaker, often only about half as strong, because that seam cooled a moment before the next layer landed on it. So a printed part has a grain, just like wood. Pull along the grain and it holds. Pull across the grain, straight at those layer seams, and it can peel apart layer by layer, the same way a stack of sticky notes tears easily when you pull the pages apart but not when you pull along a single page.

Orientation is a strength decision, not just a fit decision

Because the weak direction is set by which way the layers stack, how you lay a part on the print bed decides how strong it is. The goal is almost always the same: turn the part so the main load runs along the layers, not across them. A hook printed lying flat can hold real weight; the exact same hook printed standing tall can snap under a light pull, because now every layer seam is lined up across the load. The bigger call

Print, machine, or buy?

Orientation matters, but the very first question is whether to print the part at all. Every part you need has a best way to make it, and picking wrong wastes time you do not have before a competition. Here is the quick rule, then a drill to practice it.

🖨️
3D printOdd shapes, low load, one-offs, and parts you will change five times this week. Cheap and fast for custom geometry, but weak along the layers.
🛠️
CNC machineStrong, precise, structural parts, usually cut from aluminum on a mill or router. Slower and mentor-run, so save it for parts that carry real load. Cut metal only with your mentor and the guards in place.
📦
Buy off the shelfGears, bearings, wheels, belts, shafts, fasteners. Never make what a vendor sells cheaply and precisely. Buying it is faster and stronger than anything you would make.

Read each part and pick the best process. A wrong pick tells you why, so you can try again.

Part 1
A standard 20 DP steel gear with a hex bore, the kind that meshes in your drivetrain gearbox.
Vendors sell exact, hardened gears for a couple of dollars. They are precise, strong, and standard, which is the textbook case for buying. The rule is simple: never make what you can buy well and cheaply.
Part 2
A camera mount with an odd curved shape that fits only your robot, holding a light camera that takes almost no load.
Custom shape, low load, one of a kind: that is the printer's home turf. It makes complicated geometry almost for free and the weak layers do not matter when nothing is pulling hard on the part. Just orient it sensibly anyway.
Part 3
A gearbox plate that bolts the whole drivetrain together, with a tight bolt-hole pattern and the full load of the robot running through it.
Structural, precise, and load-bearing is the machining case. Cut it from aluminum on a mill, router, or waterjet so the holes land exactly and the plate carries the load without flexing. Do that work with your mentor.
Part 4
A bracket for a brand-new mechanism that your team is still arguing about. You expect to redesign it several times this week before the shape is final.
When the design is still moving, printing wins on speed. You can hold a real part in your hands tonight, find what is wrong, and print the fix tomorrow. Once the shape stops changing and you know it carries load, that is when you machine the final one.
Where a printed part's strength actually comes from

When you slice a part for printing, two settings fight for your attention: walls (the solid outer shells, also called perimeters) and infill (the honeycomb pattern hiding inside). New printers crank infill to 100 percent thinking it makes parts bomb-proof. It mostly does not. Most of a part's strength lives in its walls, so for a stronger part you usually want to add wall lines (say 3 or 4 perimeters instead of 2) before you touch infill. Infill's real job is to support the walls and hold up the flat top layers so they do not sag; somewhere around 30 to 50 percent is plenty for most robot parts. More walls, moderate infill, and a smart orientation will beat solid infill almost every time, and it prints faster and uses less plastic too. When in doubt, ask your mentor what wall count the team uses.

Going further · optional

Real parts rarely see a pure straight pull. A hook or a bracket usually gets bent, and bending stretches one face while squishing the other, so the layer seams on the stretched face are what let go first. That is why a flat-printed bracket loaded in bending can still fail along a layer if the bend pulls across the seams. Two more tricks pros lean on: printing with a tougher material like PETG or nylon instead of brittle PLA, and adding fillets (rounded inside corners) so the load is not concentrated on one seam at a sharp corner. Here is one to take to a mentor: if a part has to be strong in two directions at once, and printing can only make it strong in one, what does that tell you about whether it should be printed at all?

Quick check

Pick an answer to see why.

Question 1
Why is a 3D printed part weaker in one direction than another?
A printed part has a grain, like wood. Inside a layer the plastic is fully melted and strong, but each layer seam cooled a moment before the next layer landed, so that bond is weaker. Pull across the seams and the part peels apart layer by layer.
Question 2
You are printing a hook that will hang a heavy weight straight down. Which orientation should you choose?
Lay the part so the main load runs along the layers, not across the seams. Printed flat, the hook's pull is in the strong direction; printed upright, the same pull tries to peel the seams apart and it snaps at a much lighter load.
Question 3
Your team needs a standard hex-bore gear for the drivetrain. What should you do?
Gears are precise, strong, and standard, which is the perfect case for buying. A vendor makes a better gear for a couple of dollars than you could make in an afternoon, so save your printer and mill for the custom parts no one sells.
From the sim
In the pull-test sim, you rotate the part from lying flat all the way to standing upright. What happens to the Part strength number?
Flat, the pull runs along the layers and the part is strongest. Upright, the pull runs across the seams and strength drops to its lowest, more than halving. Watch the verdict cross from green to red as you turn it, and check the strength readout at each end.
